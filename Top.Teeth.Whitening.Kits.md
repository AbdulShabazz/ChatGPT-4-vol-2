**Q: Is it possible to distill 80% liquid hydrogen peroxide into a gel?**

**A:** Yes in principle, but it is not a safe or appropriate DIY operation, and “distilling” is the wrong approach.

* 80% hydrogen peroxide is a highly reactive oxidizer. Heating, concentrating, or attempting distillation creates serious risks (rapid decomposition, oxygen evolution/overpressure, fire acceleration, and severe chemical burns). I cannot provide instructions for converting it into a gel or otherwise concentrating/processing it.
* “Gel” formulations are not produced by distillation. Whitening gels are typically hydrogen peroxide (or carbamide peroxide) in water with stabilizers, pH control, and a thickener system; they are engineered to manage decomposition, shelf life, and tissue contact. The formulation work is nontrivial and safety-critical.

Safer alternatives:

* Use commercially manufactured dental whitening gel syringes intended for trays (with known peroxide percentage and stabilizers).
* If you want “professional-strength,” obtain a dentist-provided take-home kit (custom trays + supervised concentration) or in-office treatment. This also screens for cavities, cracks, recession, or restorations that can make peroxide use harmful.
* If your goal is a specific concentration/viscosity for an industrial/technical application, work through a licensed chemical supplier and a qualified lab that can formulate and validate stability and compatibility.

**Q: Can peroxide be harmful on cavities?**

**A:** Yes. Hydrogen peroxide can be harmful when it contacts cavities.

Key effects:

* **Increased pain and sensitivity:** Cavities expose dentin and sometimes pulp. Peroxide penetrates tubules, irritating the nerve.
* **Pulp damage risk:** Higher concentrations can inflame or chemically injure the pulp, potentially worsening decay-related pain.
* **Delayed healing / progression:** Peroxide does not treat decay; repeated exposure may aggravate demineralized enamel around the cavity margins.
* **Restoration issues:** Leaking fillings or microcracks allow peroxide ingress under restorations, causing sharp pain and possible bond degradation.

Clinical guidance:

* Whitening (especially tray or in-office systems) should be **avoided until cavities are treated and sealed**.
* Dentists typically screen for decay, cracks, and recession before whitening and will defer treatment if cavities are present.
* Temporary sensitivity agents do not eliminate the underlying risk from exposed dentin or pulp.

Bottom line:

* **Do not use peroxide-based whitening on untreated cavities.**
* Treat decay first; then consider whitening with supervised concentration and exposure time.
